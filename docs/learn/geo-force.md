# Geo-Force

We define the Geometric Force as a discrete vector field \(\mathbf{F} : \Lambda \to \mathbb{R}^{3}\) on a pixel grid \(\Lambda = \{1, \dots, h\} \times \{1, \dots, w\}\), where \(\mathbf{F}(a, b)\) is the force vector at pixel \((a, b)\). It is a virtual force, evaluated at a frozen *geometric configuration*, by which we mean the pair \((\mathbf{O}, \mathcal{P})\) of a visual input cast as a point cloud \(\mathbf{O} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times 3}\) of a rigid object and the pose \(\mathcal{P} = (\mathbf{R}, \mathbf{t}) \in \mathrm{SE}(3)\) of a virtual touch sensor, both recorded at the same instant. All quantities are expressed in a fixed world frame. In this frame, \(\mathbf{t} \in \mathbb{R}^{3}\) is the position of the origin of the sensor's viewing plane and \(\mathbf{R} = [\mathbf{e}_u \; \mathbf{e}_v \; \mathbf{n}]\) the sensor orientation, where the unit vectors \(\mathbf{e}_u, \mathbf{e}_v\) span the viewing plane and \(\mathbf{n}\) is its unit normal. The vector \(\mathbf{F}(a, b)\) is located at the point \(\mathbf{x}(a, b) = \mathbf{t} + u_a \mathbf{e}_u + v_b \mathbf{e}_v\), where \((u_a, v_b)\) are the coordinates of pixel \((a, b)\) in the viewing plane. The Geometric Force is a function of the geometric configuration alone, \(\mathbf{F} = \Phi(\mathbf{O}, \mathcal{P})\), where \(\Phi\) is a nonlinear map composed of three maps, \(\Phi = \Phi_{\text{force}} \circ \Phi_{\text{touch}} \circ \Phi_{\text{render}}\). The first map, \(\Phi_{\text{render}}\), renders the geometric configuration into a depth patch \(\mathbf{D} : \Lambda \to \mathbb{R}\) of the object as seen from the sensor's viewing plane. This step is needed because, whenever the sensor is in contact with the object, the touched part is occluded from direct view. The second map, \(\Phi_{\text{touch}}\), predicts from \(\mathbf{D}\) a touch signal \(\mathbf{S} : \Lambda \to \mathbb{R}^{3}\), an RGB tactile image that encodes the interaction as the disturbance of the sensor's contact area. It is the central component of \(\Phi\): learned from real touch data, it grounds the purely geometric input in the physical response of the sensor. The third map, \(\Phi_{\text{force}}\), estimates the force \(\mathbf{F}\) from \(\mathbf{S}\). If the sensor is not in contact with the object at the given geometric configuration, \(\mathbf{S}\) shows no disturbance and \(\mathbf{F}\) vanishes. By construction, \(\mathbf{D}\), \(\mathbf{S}\) and \(\mathbf{F}\) share the same pixel grid \(\Lambda\), i.e. \(\mathbf{D} \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times w}\) and \(\mathbf{S}, \mathbf{F} \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times w \times 3}\). We define the resolution \(r\) of the point cloud as the minimum distance between its points, \(r = \min_{i \neq j} \|\mathbf{o}_i - \mathbf{o}_j\|\), where \(\mathbf{o}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3}\) denotes the \(i\)-th point of \(\mathbf{O}\). The resolution determines the level of detail in \(\mathbf{D}\), and thereby affects \(\mathbf{S}\) and, in turn, \(\mathbf{F}\). In this work, we are concerned with the force component normal to the viewing plane, \(F^{\perp}(a, b) = \mathbf{F}(a, b) \cdot \mathbf{n}\).
